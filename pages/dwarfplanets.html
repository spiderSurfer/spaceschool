Dwarf Planets

What Defines a Planet? In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) established three specific criteria that a celestial body must meet to be considered a full-scale planet: Orbits the Sun: It must revolve around our host star. Spherical Shape: It must be large enough that its own gravity has pulled it into a nearly round shape. Cleared its Neighborhood: It must be the dominant gravitational force in its orbit, having cleared away other objects of similar size. What is a Dwarf Planet? A dwarf planet meets the first two criteria—it orbits the Sun and is nearly round—but it fails the third. Dwarf planets lack the gravitational "muscle" to clear their orbital paths of debris and other small objects. This is the primary reason Pluto was reclassified from a planet to a dwarf planet. The Five Recognized Dwarf Planets While there may be hundreds of dwarf planets in our solar system yet to be discovered, the IAU currently recognizes these five, listed here by their distance from the Sun: Ceres: Located in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter; it is the smallest of the recognized dwarf planets. Pluto: Once the ninth planet, it resides in the Kuiper Belt, a region of icy objects beyond Neptune. Haumea: Located in the Kuiper Belt, it is unique for its elongated, football-like shape and rapid rotation. Makemake: Also in the Kuiper Belt; it is one of the brightest objects in the outer solar system. Eris: One of the largest dwarf planets, its discovery in the region beyond the Kuiper Belt prompted the debate that led to the new definition of a planet.
